Autumn

A season of change, memory and acceptance.

SEASONS OF THE SOUL • 01

Autumn

All good things come to a bitter end.
The trees shed their precious leaves,
the birds fly for distant miles
in a desperate search
for a home of comfort.

The fearsome bear yields,
for it knows the times to come are cruel.
The largest and smallest of creatures
bend beneath
the coming cruelty.

Yet the earth remains still warm,
though not as it was before the beginning.
It is not the end—
only a season
deceiving the blinded.

For who among us
can truly know what comes next?

We walk beneath changing skies
with no promise of tomorrow,
and still we move forward,
trying, failing,
loving what may one day leave us.

Perhaps that is why we hold each other
so tightly—
because grief is not born
from losing what meant nothing,
but from losing what once felt
like home.

The human heart and soul,
blinded by love,
move through the seasons:
the calm before the storm,
the pain to come,
distant yet real.

We desire peace,
yet carry the means to destroy it.
We pray for gentle hands,
yet our own hands
are capable of setting the world ablaze.

We hate the suffering
we are willing to create.

Perhaps it is because
we fear what we cannot understand.
Perhaps it is because
we cannot bear to stand still
while the future remains unknown.

So we try.

We build.
We break.
We forgive.
We abandon.
We love.

And sometimes,
we hurt the very people
whose hearts beat
with the same fear as ours.

Do they not grieve as we grieve?
Do they not love as we love?
Are we not all
mere men among men,
carrying fragile hearts
through a world none of us
were given the power to understand?

We tell ourselves lies
because the truth is uncertain.

We choose to remain blind
because seeing the storm ahead
does not grant us the power
to stop it.

Yet somewhere within,
we all know what is to come.

And still,
we hope.

Still,
we love.

Still,
we reach for one another
beneath a sky
that could change at any moment.

Perhaps that is humanity's greatest contradiction:

We long for peace
while preparing for war.

We fear the flames
yet sometimes become the fire.

We know that everything ends,
yet we love as though
it never will.

The seasons change.
The leaves fall.
The earth grows cold
and warm again.

And the human heart—
knowing nothing of tomorrow,
afraid of everything it may lose,
still choosing to love—

remains
beautifully naive.

Summer

Fire, intensity and the things within us.

SEASONS OF THE SOUL • 02

Summer

```

The fields are heavy with harvest,
golden beneath the summer sun.
The earth gives freely,
and the hands that once planted
now gather what they have grown.

The rivers run gently,
the winds carry laughter,
and children chase one another
beneath a sky untouched by war.

What a beautiful thing—
to be young enough
to believe the world
will always remain this way.

A child looks toward the table,
counting the faces around it.
Mother.
Father.
Brother.
Sister.

And somewhere within that small heart
comes a quiet thought:

Will we always eat together?
Will Father always come home?
Will Mother always laugh like this?
Will tomorrow look like today?

The child does not know
that peace is not promised.
So they cherish the moment
without knowing
how precious it is.

They run through the fields,
their laughter rising with the birds,
believing happiness
is something that simply exists.

And perhaps it should be.

But is it not strange
how easily we call peace ordinary
when there are those
who would give everything
to hear a child's laughter again?

The parent watches from the doorway,
smiling at the little things:
muddy feet,
crooked smiles,
arguments forgotten before sunset.

They have worked for this harvest.
They have prayed for this peace.
They have endured the seasons
so their children would not have to.

Yet even while watching,
a fear remains hidden beneath the joy.

What if tomorrow takes it all?

What if the house becomes empty?
What if the laughter becomes silence?
What if the hands they hold today
become memories tomorrow?

The parent knows what the child does not:

Peace is beautiful
precisely because it can be lost.

And still,
the human heart is selfish.

We want more.
More food.
More comfort.
More years.
More laughter around the table.

Is that selfishness,
or simply the soul
refusing to let go
of what it loves?

Somewhere beyond the fields,
another child watches the same summer sky.

But there is no mother calling them home.
No father waiting at the door.
No familiar voice
asking how their day was.

They remember another summer—
before the war took everything.

Before the house was silent.
Before the names they loved
became names they could only remember.

The harvest grows,
children laugh,
families gather,
and the world continues.

But what does abundance mean
to someone who has nothing left to gather?

What does peace mean
to the child
who learned too early
that peace can disappear?

Perhaps that is the cruelty of summer:
it reminds us of everything
worth protecting.

The golden fields.
The warm homes.
The voices around the table.
The innocent laughter of children.

We call these things ordinary
because we are fortunate enough
to have them.

But should we?

How many people must lose their homes
before we understand what a home means?
How many children must lose their families
before we cherish our own?
How much peace must be destroyed
before we learn to protect it?

The child wants only
for everyone to remain together.

The parent wants only
for the child to grow old
without learning the language of war.

The orphan wants only
what the others have:
someone to come home to.

Three souls beneath the same sun,
wanting different things,
yet bound by the same human longing—
to love,
to be loved,
and to keep what we cannot control.

Perhaps the human soul
is not measured
by how much it possesses,
but by how deeply
it cherishes what it has.

Summer will end.
The harvest will be gathered.
The children will grow.
The parents will age.

And one day,
even this peace
will become a memory.

So while the sun still shines,
while the table is full,
while the laughter still fills the house—

Why do we wait for loss
to teach us gratitude?

Why do we destroy
what we spend our lives
praying to keep?

Perhaps this is the human soul:
selfish enough to want forever,
fragile enough to fear tomorrow,
yet capable of finding joy
in a single summer evening
spent beside the people
it calls home.

And perhaps,
for one moment,
that should be enough.

03

Dawn-Dusk

Beneath the surface, another world waits.

SEASONS OF THE SOUL • 03

Dawn - Dusk

Dawn does not arrive.
It is earned,
the way anything earned
is given slowly,
in a color we don't notice
until it is already morning.

Somewhere a mother wakes first,
counting heartbeats
before she counts anything else—
who is breathing,
who is still beside her,
who the night did not take.

She has learned to do this quietly,
so the fear does not wake the others.
So the house still feels
like a house,
and not like a wound
waiting to reopen.

The light is young here.
It does not yet know
what the day will ask of it.
Neither do we.

Somewhere else,
a soldier watches the same light
rise over unfamiliar ground,
and wonders if his mother
is counting heartbeats too,
two thousand miles
and one prayer away.

He does not tell her
what the night was like.
Some things are too heavy
to send home
in a letter.

By midmorning
the fear is only a rumor
we tell ourselves
to stay alert,
not yet a certainty—
though certainty
is always closer
than we admit.

A child waits by the window,
not for anything in particular,
just waiting,
the way children wait
when they have learned
that people they love
do not always come home
on time.

Noon is the hardest light.
It shows us
exactly what we are,
no shadow to hide in,
no excuse for looking away
from what we've done
or failed to do.

This is when the work is done.
Not the easy work of hoping—
the other kind.
The kind that calluses the hand
and softens nothing.
The kind no one applauds.

We ache by early afternoon.
Every day carries
a small war in it,
a short and private battle
we cross alone,
even surrounded by people
who love us.

And still—
we cross it.

We do not always know why.
Perhaps because stopping
feels like surrender.
Perhaps because somewhere
a child is still waiting
by a window,
and waiting deserves
an answer.

By late afternoon
the light turns generous,
forgiving the harsh work of noon,
gilding the same hands
that hardened an hour before.

A father comes home.
Not every father.
Not every day.
But this one, this time,
the door opens
and it is him.

A child counts the faces
at the table again,
relieved without knowing why
relief was necessary—
too young still
to understand
how easily
a table can go quiet.

Somewhere else,
a door does not open.
A table stays
one place short.
A mother sets it anyway,
out of habit,
or hope,
or something
she cannot yet name
as grief.

We do not speak of this enough—
how one house can fill with laughter
while another, down the same road,
fills only with silence,
and the sun does not know
the difference.
It warms them both the same.

Dusk comes like a widow's hand
finally warm again,
like a child's laugh
grown fluent,
like a soldier's letter
arriving three weeks late
and still, somehow,
exactly on time.

Color spills across the sky
the way blood once spilled
across the frozen wasteland—
except now it means
something closer to grace,
something closer
to being forgiven
for surviving
when others did not.

We were clay, fired and softened.
We were frost, and then the thaw.
We were the wound
and the hand
that pressed against it.

Now we are only this—
tired,
warm,
still standing,
still breathing,
still reaching for the ones beside us
as if reaching
were the only prayer
we know how to finish.

Night will come,
as it always does.
But we no longer flinch from it
the way we once did.

We have learned
that darkness is not always winter.
Sometimes it is only rest,
the earth exhaling,
the sky closing its one warm eye
so it can open it again
tomorrow,
on someone else's dawn,
someone else's ache,
someone else's table
finally, mercifully, full.

Dawn to dusk—
this is the whole of it.

Not a war against the cold.
Not a debt owed to the light.
Not a promise that tomorrow
will look like today.

Only a single day,
carried honestly,
by every hand willing to carry it—
the mother's, the soldier's,
the child's at the window,
the widow's, the father's,
ours—

from one soft dark
into another,
and somehow,
still grateful
to have made it
this far.

Winter

Stillness, silence and frozen memories.

SEASONS OF THE SOUL • 04

Winter

The times are tough.
The trees stand barren,
their branches reaching
toward a sky that gives nothing.

The land lies buried
beneath snow and frost.
The mighty glacier
swallows the sad and meek.

Snow blankets the earth,
hiding the sins of the dark.
Death looms over the soft-hearted,
while crimson rivers
cross the frozen wasteland.

Hearts turn to stone—
cold and lifeless.
Fangs sink into the prideful
and the slothful.

Hunger devours the gluttonous.
The selfish lay down,
alone and defeated.

The wind roars.
Death runs rampant.
Hunger and thirst—
killers of all.

May mercy
be shown to the kind soul.

The kindred souls
comfort one another.
The widow longs
for the touch of her lover.
The child grieves
for the days of peace and joy,
now distant and gone.

The cold reflects
in the eyes
of those once dearest.

Wrath overcomes love.
We turn against the small and weak.
We inflict upon others
the pain we carry within ourselves.

The human soul—
frozen by a sorrow
it was never meant to hold.

The world we live in
freezes what was once warm.
Clouds of hatred and fear
block out the sun.

To survive,
we become the monsters.
We burn what threatens us
to protect what remains.

Can you really blame us?

We were born warm and gentle.
Now we are brutal and cold.

In this world,
survival drives us
to abandon
our humanity as a whole.

Did we choose this?

Were we not clay,
molded by the hands
of the world?

Were we not shaped
by every wound,
every loss,
every winter
we were forced to endure?

Perhaps the cruelest thing
about winter
is not the cold.

It is what the cold
teaches us to become.

And when spring finally comes,
when the frost begins to fade,
will there still be warmth
left within us?

Or will we have frozen so deeply
that even the sun
can no longer reach the soul?

Spring

Beauty, impermanence and new beginnings.

SEASONS OF THE SOUL • 05

Spring

The frost releases
its grip, slow and unsure,
as if even the cold
forgets how to hold on.

The sky remembers blue.
Light returns,
not as a stranger,
but as something we once knew.

The glacier weeps—
not in defeat,
but in release.
What it swallowed all winter
it slowly gives back.

Green pushes through
the wreckage of white.
It does not ask permission.
It simply insists on living.

The soil, once a grave,
becomes a cradle.
What we buried
did not all die.
Some of it was only sleeping.

Rivers unfreeze
and remember their names.
They carry the crimson away,
diluting it in miles of current
until it is only water again.

We asked, when winter ended,
if any warmth would be left in us.

Here is the answer:
warmth does not return whole.
It returns in pieces—
a hand unclenched,
a door left unlocked,
a stranger offered bread
before we ask what they did
to survive.

The widow steps outside.
The sun does not undo her loss.
It only warms the shoulders
that carry it now.

The child laughs once,
uncertain at first—
like someone remembering
a language they were told to forget.

We do not become gentle again
all at once.
The claws do not vanish.
We only learn, slowly,
to open the hand
that made them.

Perhaps this is what spring is—
not innocence returned,
but the choice, made again,
to be soft in a world
that taught us otherwise.

We were clay once.
Winter fired us hard.
But even fired clay
can hold water,
can hold flowers,
can hold something
other than survival.

The wounds do not close.
They bloom instead—
strange flowers
growing from the places
that once only ached.

Can you really blame the earth
for trying again?

Everything that survived
is still here,
reaching—
not because it has forgotten
what the cold taught it,
but because it remembers
something older:

that it was born to grow.

And when summer comes,
when the thaw is finished
and the frost is only memory,
will we recognize
the warmth as ours—
or will we flinch,
still waiting
for winter to return?

Perhaps that, too,
is part of spring:
learning to trust
the sun again.
